# The Math: Frontal Curvature and Rocking Period

## Why derive this at all

The toroidal foot lets $R_f$ (frontal-plane curvature) be changed independently of $R_s$
(sagittal-plane curvature). But solving the *full* coupled 3D dynamics between the two
planes for a non-spherical foot remains an open technical challenge — nobody has a clean
closed-form model for it yet. So rather than trying to model everything at once, this
derivation isolates one piece that can be solved in closed form: the **timing** of
walking, generated by the oscillating rocking motion, characterized by the rocking
period. The question answered here is narrow and specific: model rocking on a level
surface, and find how $R_f$ alone affects that rocking period through energy
conservation between foot collisions — then check the prediction against the real
rocking period measured during actual gait on the slope.

**Assumptions:** the feet roll without slipping, the walker is treated as a single rigid
body, energy is conserved between collisions, and the center of mass sits below the
center of curvature of the frontal-plane circle (a statically stable configuration).

## Setting up the energy

As the walker rocks by rolling over the foot, its center of mass moves due to both
rotation and translation together. With $\theta$ the rocking angle from vertical, $R_f$
the frontal radius, and $h$ the distance from the foot's center of curvature to the
center of mass:

\[
x_G = R_f\theta - h\sin\theta, \qquad y_G = R_f - h\cos\theta
\]

Differentiating gives the COM velocity, and from there the kinetic and potential
energies:

\[
\dot{x}_G = (R_f - h\cos\theta)\dot\theta
\]
\[
\dot{y}_G = (h\sin\theta)\dot\theta
\]
\[
V = mg(R_f - h\cos\theta)
\]
\[
K = \frac{1}{2}\left[I_G + m\left(R_f^2 + h^2 - 2R_f h\cos\theta\right)\right]\dot\theta^2
\]

where $I_G$ is the walker's rotational inertia about its own center of mass. Because the
normal force and friction act at the contact point — which has zero velocity relative to
the ground during rolling without slipping — neither force does any work. Gravity is the
only force doing work on the system, so total energy $E = K + V$ stays constant
throughout smooth rocking.

## Linearizing

To get a linear equation of motion, small-angle approximations are applied near
$\theta = 0$: $\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2$ for the potential-energy term, and the
cruder $\cos\theta \approx 1$ for the rotational-inertia term (justified below). This
reduces the potential energy to a parabola in $\theta$ and the inertia term to a
constant:

\[
V \approx mg\left[R_f - h\left(1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2}\right)\right] = mg(R_f - h) + \frac{1}{2}mgh\theta^2
\]

Let $h_0 = R_f - h$ denote the walker's standing height above the ground:

\[
V \approx mgh_0 + \frac{1}{2}mg(R_f - h_0)\theta^2
\]

Since $\cos\theta$ only appears in the coefficient of $\dot\theta^2$ in the kinetic
energy, the cruder $\cos\theta \approx 1$ is sufficient there:

\[
K \approx \frac{1}{2}\left[I_G + m(R_f - h)^2\right]\dot\theta^2
\]

By the parallel-axis theorem, the moment of inertia about the foot-ground contact point
is $I_C = I_G + mh_0^2$, giving total energy:

\[
E = \frac{1}{2}I_C\dot\theta^2 + \frac{1}{2}mg(R_f - h_0)\theta^2 + mgh_0
\]

## From conserved energy to a period

Since $E$ is conserved, $\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$:

\[
I_C\dot\theta\ddot\theta + mg(R_f - h_0)\theta\dot\theta = 0
\]

Canceling the common factor $\dot\theta$:

\[
\ddot\theta + \frac{mg(R_f - h_0)}{I_C}\theta = 0
\]

This is simple harmonic motion, with period:

\[
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I_C}{mg(R_f - h_0)}}
\]

Comparing two frontal radii $R_1$ and $R_2$ on otherwise identical robots — same $m$,
$I_C$, and $h_0$ — the mass and inertia terms cancel entirely, leaving a relation that
depends on geometry alone:

\[
\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \sqrt{\frac{R_1 - h_0}{R_2 - h_0}}
\]

$R_2 > R_1$ predicts $T_2 < T_1$: a larger frontal radius of curvature produces a shorter
rocking period. This is checked directly against physical trial data on the
[results page](/research/results/), where predicted and measured ratios differ by at most 1.0%
across all six radius pairings tested.

## How good is the small-angle approximation, really

The lateral tilt angle is physically bounded by the foot's actual arc length. Using the
smallest $R_f$ tested experimentally (110 mm), the maximum tilt angle works out to

\[
\theta_{max} = 0.452 \text{ rad}
\]

At this maximum tilt, the second-order cosine approximation applied to the potential
energy (Eq. for $V$) yields a relative truncation error of **1.72%** in the change in
gravitational potential energy — small enough to support using it in the linearization,
and directly measured from the foot's own geometry rather than assumed from a generic
convention.

## What this derivation does not claim

This is a small-angle approximation of the rocking timing on a level surface — it
deliberately does not model swing-leg inertia coupling, energy losses from foot
collisions, or yaw motion, all of which are present during actual gait on the slope. The
fact that the prediction still matches measured rocking periods to within 1% (see
[Results](/research/results/)) suggests these unmodeled dynamics don't substantially change the
$R_f$–period relationship over the conditions tested — but that's an empirical finding
from the data, not something built into the derivation itself.

$R_s$, notably, doesn't appear anywhere in this derivation — it's a frontal-plane-only
result. Whether and how $R_s$ affects timing is an open, purely empirical question,
covered on the results page linked below.
